Big Idea

Why Do Real Programmers "Freeze" Their Features?

? Think about it
  • If you keep adding new features right up until showcase day, what usually happens to the old features?
  • Would you rather show 3 features that work perfectly, or 6 features where half are broken?
Real software teams pick a "freeze date" — after that, no new features, only fixes. It protects the parts that already work.

Vocabulary + Process

A Bug Hunter's Four Steps

1
Describe it.

What should happen? What actually happens instead?

2
Hunt it.

Read the code near where things went wrong — line by line.

3
Test small fixes.

Change one thing at a time and re-run to see what changes.

4
Document it.

Write down what was wrong and how you fixed it, for next time.

Peer Testing

Why Does Someone ELSE Need to Test Your Project?

Think like a scientist

You know exactly how to click your own project the "right" way — so you never trigger the hidden bug.

A partner doesn't know your secret shortcuts. They'll click the "wrong" button — and that's exactly how real bugs get found.

Today's job: swap projects with a partner. Each of you tries to break the other's project — then fix your top 2 bugs.

Sort It Out

Which Bugs Get Fixed First? (click a card to flip)

You can't fix everything before showcase. Flip each card — is it a must-fix or a nice-to-have?

The whole program crashes when you click "Start"

MUST-FIX 🚨
Nothing works until this is solved.

The background color isn't your favorite shade of purple

NICE-TO-HAVE 🎨
Looks fine — save it for polish day.

The chatbot never replies, no matter what you type

MUST-FIX 🚨
The core feature has to actually work.

🤖 AI Moment

Bug-Testing the AI Part Too

A chatbot or classifier can have "bugs" that aren't about crashing — it can be confusing or unfair and still "work."

? Ask your partner while they test
  • Could someone misunderstand what my AI part is trying to say?
  • Does it treat every kind of input fairly, or does it only work for certain examples?
Fixing an AI "fairness bug" counts just as much as fixing a crash — write it on your bug list too.

Coming Up

Write Your 30-Second "What My Project Does" Intro

Every great demo starts with a short, clear introduction. Yours should answer three things in about 30 seconds:

  • 1️⃣ What is my project called, and what does it do?
  • 2️⃣ What's the AI part, in one sentence?
  • 3️⃣ What's one thing I'm proud of?
Say it out loud once before next class — even to a pet or a mirror counts!

Why Practice Talking About Your Project Before the Real Showcase?

? Think about it
  • Have you ever felt nervous doing something the very first time, but calmer the second time?
  • What's the difference between practicing alone and practicing with one friendly partner?
Rehearsal isn't a test — it's a warm-up. Every performer, athlete, and scientist practices before the "real thing."

Presenter Toolkit

Four Habits of a Calm, Clear Presenter

😊
Smile & make eye contact.

It tells your audience "I'm ready and I'm friendly."

🐢
Slow down.

Nervous talkers speed up — pause between ideas instead.

🗂️
Beginning, middle, end.

What it is → how it works → what you're proud of.

🎯
Show, don't just tell.

Click a button while you talk — let the project speak too.

Rehearsal Ladder

Start Small, Build Confidence — Never Cold-Called

Presenting is always opt-in. You choose how far up the ladder you climb today.

Step 1

"Show your neighbor" — one partner, one project, no audience pressure.

Step 2

Swap roles — now you listen and give one kind, specific piece of feedback.

Step 3 (optional)

Volunteers show a whole table — practice with a slightly bigger, still-friendly audience.

🤖 AI Moment

Explain Your AI Idea in ONE Sentence

The hardest part of any demo is explaining the "smart" part simply. Try filling in this sentence with your partner:

"My project uses AI to ______, by learning from ______."

Example: "My project uses AI to guess if a message is happy or sad, by learning from example sentences I trained it on."
