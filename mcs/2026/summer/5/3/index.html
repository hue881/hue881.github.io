💡 Mini-Lesson · Click each step

Real AI isn't magic — it's a loop

1
2
3

📸

Step 1 — Features & Labels

Every example is broken into features (measurable clues — ear shape, pixel brightness, whisker length) paired with a label (the correct answer, like "cat"). A model with 10,000 labeled photos might track thousands of tiny features at once.

🔍

Step 2 — Weights

The model assigns a numeric weight to each feature — how much it should count. Pointy ears might get weight +0.8 toward "cat," floppy ears -0.6. Training is really just adjusting millions of these numbers.

🎯

Step 3 — Confidence Score

The model doesn't just say "cat" — it outputs a confidence (e.g. 87% cat, 13% dog). Anything below a threshold can be flagged "uncertain" instead of guessed.

The training loop, more precisely:

  • Predict — run the current weights on a training example
  • Measure error — compare the guess to the true label (this gap is called loss)
  • Adjust weights — nudge weights to shrink that error, repeat across the whole dataset dozens of times (each full pass is an epoch)

👆 Click the numbered dots above to walk through each step. Ask: what happens if the model memorizes training photos instead of learning general patterns? (That's called overfitting.)

🤖 AI Moment · The Catch

What if the training data is unfair?

Remember the warm-up puzzle — cats were only white and dogs were only brown?

👉 Click here to reveal what the AI would wrongly learn ▾

The AI would think every white animal is a cat! This is called bias: when a model makes unfair or wrong guesses because the data it learned from didn't show the whole picture.

🌍 Harder: a real-world case ▾

Click to reveal a true story about facial-recognition AI.

Real facial-recognition systems trained mostly on lighter-skinned faces were measured to make far more errors on darker-skinned faces — in one study, error rates were over 30x higher for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men. Discuss: if this system is used for security or hiring, who is put at risk, and whose responsibility is it to fix — the coder, the company, or the data collectors?

confidence

Click →

The model's own estimate (e.g. 87%) of how sure it is about a prediction.

Click →

🛠 Guided Build · 20 min

Let's break our own classifier — on purpose

Step by step, together:

  1. Open Teachable Machine's Image Project.
  2. Train Class A with only your own hand in one pose, lighting, background.
  3. Train Class B the same limited way.
  4. Test it with a different student's hand or a new background.

🛠 What we should see

Watch it misclassify — that's the whole point

Because we only trained on one person's hand, the model overfit to narrow features (this exact skin tone, lighting, background) instead of learning what makes a hand a hand in general.

This gap between accuracy on training data vs. new data is called the generalization gap — the smaller it is, the more trustworthy the model.

Debate with your table — no easy answers:

  • Was the model "broken," or did it do exactly what its data taught it?
  • If a company only has data from 10 volunteers, is it ethical to ship the product anyway? Why or why not?
  • Could you ever collect training data with ZERO bias? Defend your answer.
  • Estimate: if 1 person's hand ≈ 20 photos, how many photos might you need from 15 different people to fairly cover the class?

1) Deliberately train a classifier with a sneaky bias (e.g., only smiling faces = "happy"). Test it on a neutral face.
2) Now design a fix: what THREE specific categories of new data would you add, and in what order, to reduce the bias fastest?
3) Explain why order might matter.
